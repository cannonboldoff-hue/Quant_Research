Abstract

We quantify how much machine learning (ML) adds to 39 established trend-following rules and identify the conditions under which the improvement is statistically robust. ML is inserted at five separately evaluated stages of the trading pipeline (trade filtering, regime gating, position sizing, exit timing and parameter adaptation), with five model families per stage selected on a validation block that precedes each test year in a purged, expanding walk-forward design. On 174 instruments (equity indices, futures, currencies, cryptocurrencies and ETFs) traded out of sample from January 2006 to October 2026 with next-open execution, asset-class transaction costs and volatility targeting, the rule-based baselines earn a mean Sharpe ratio of 0.01. ML trade filtering and ML position sizing raise it by 0.47 and 0.44 on average, with significant improvements for 31 and 33 of the 39 strategies after Benjamini–Hochberg correction. ML exits add 0.28, parameter adaptation 0.16 and a strategy-agnostic regime gate nothing. The gains survive a random-prediction placebo, a random-walk null experiment, alternative validation designs, every five-year sub-period and zero to triple transaction costs. They are absent for G10 currencies and, for position sizing, at intraday cryptocurrency frequencies. A feature ablation shows that the improvement does not require technical indicators: a model that sees only volatility and the state of the open trade matches a model given 56 indicators. ML therefore acts as a learned trade-management layer rather than as an extractor of indicator information. The improvements are statistically robust, but most ML-enhanced rules remain only marginally profitable on their own.

Keywords: trend following; technical trading rules; machine learning; meta-labelling; walk-forward validation; backtest overfitting; multiple hypothesis testing; placebo test.

1. Introduction

Trend following is among the oldest and best-documented systematic investment styles. Its building blocks (moving-average crossovers, channel breakouts, volatility stops and time-series momentum) are simple, public and, in most cases, several decades old (Donchian 1960; Wilder 1978; Brock et al. 1992; Moskowitz et al. 2012; Hurst et al. 2017). Over the past decade it has become routine to bolt machine learning onto such rules: classifiers filter signals, regime models switch strategies on and off, and predicted probabilities set position sizes (López de Prado 2018; Lim et al. 2019; Joubert 2022). The value of doing so is, however, rarely measured against the rule itself under one controlled protocol. Academic studies typically report the profitability of an ML strategy in isolation, and practitioner evidence usually consists of a filter that “helped” on the instrument on which it was tuned. Neither answers the question that matters to someone who already trades a trend rule: what does ML add to it, and can that addition be trusted?

This paper asks that question directly:

How much does ML improve established trend-following strategies over their original rule-based implementations, and under what market, instrument, strategy, indicator, model and validation conditions does the improvement remain statistically significant and robust?

We answer it with a controlled, stage-by-stage design. Thirty-nine published or widely used trend-following rules are implemented faithfully as baselines. ML is then inserted at five distinct points of the trading pipeline (trade selection, trend-regime detection, position sizing, exit timing and parameter adaptation), and each insertion is evaluated on its own against the same baseline on identical days. At every stage, five model families compete (regularised logistic regression, random forest, extremely randomised trees, gradient boosting and a neural network); the winner is chosen only on a validation block that precedes each out-of-sample year, and every alternative is reported as well. The test bed is a public multi-asset daily panel of 174 instruments across equity indices, futures, currencies, cryptocurrencies and ETFs, with robustness panels at one-hour and four-hour frequency and a deliberately survivorship-biased single-stock universe that is labelled as such.

Because the design involves many comparisons (39 strategies × 5 stages × 5 models, plus backtest variants), the inference accounts explicitly for the size of the search: paired block-bootstrap and HAC tests of Sharpe-ratio differences, Holm and Benjamini–Hochberg adjustment across the 195 strategy–stage tests, Hansen’s test of superior predictive ability and White’s Reality Check within each strategy, Romano–Wolf stepdown p-values, the probability of backtest overfitting and the deflated Sharpe ratio. We add three controls that are, to our knowledge, rarely used together in this literature: (i) a placebo that applies each ML decision rule with random numbers instead of model predictions, which isolates the information contributed by the model from the mechanical effect of intervening; (ii) a null experiment that runs the entire pipeline unchanged on synthetic random-walk markets as a screen for look-ahead leakage; and (iii) a feature ablation that asks which inputs carry the improvement.

Our main findings are as follows. First, where ML is inserted matters more than which model is used. Trade filtering (meta-labelling) and position sizing raise the out-of-sample Sharpe ratio of the average rule by 0.47 and 0.44, from a baseline of essentially zero, and the improvement is significant for 31 and 33 of the 39 rules after false-discovery-rate control. ML exits help less reliably (+0.28), parameter adaptation little (+0.16), and a strategy-agnostic trend-regime gate does not help at all. Second, the improvements are not an artefact of trading less: the placebo is negative or null at every stage, and the random-walk experiment produces no broad improvement. Third, the gains are robust to the validation design, the time period and the backtest assumptions, but not to the sampling frequency or to the most efficiently priced market (G10 currencies). Fourth, ML mainly repairs weak rules: the benefit falls sharply with the rule’s own Sharpe ratio. Fifth, and most importantly for interpretation, the improvement does not depend on the technical indicators at all. A model that sees only recent volatility and the state of the open trade performs as well as one that sees 56 indicators. Finally, a statistically robust improvement is not the same as a profitable strategy: the absolute Sharpe ratio of the best ML configuration survives deflation for only 9 of the 39 rules.

The paper makes four contributions. (1) It provides a controlled, stage-by-stage measurement of the incremental value of ML for a large, diverse set of published trend rules, under one protocol and with honest out-of-sample model selection. (2) It reports the full search (every model, every stage, every backtest variant) together with multiple-testing and overfitting diagnostics, rather than only the best configuration. (3) It introduces a placebo control and a feature ablation that separate learned information from mechanical intervention and identify the source of the gain. (4) It releases a modular, tested framework in which each experiment is recorded in a registry that links strategy, indicators, market, instrument, dataset, ML stage, model, validation design and backtest configuration to its result.

2. Related literature

Trend following and technical rules. Systematic trend following has a long empirical record. Donchian’s channel-breakout rule (1960) and the moving-average and trading-range-break rules tested by Brock et al. (1992) are the canonical technical rules. Moskowitz et al. (2012) document time-series momentum across 58 futures and forward contracts, and Hurst et al. (2017) extend the evidence to more than a century of data. Levine and Pedersen (2016) show that moving-average crossovers and time-series momentum are closely related, weighting past returns differently. Simple tactical rules such as Faber (2007)’s ten-month moving-average filter and Antonacci (2014)’s absolute momentum are widely followed by practitioners. Sullivan et al. (1999) showed that the apparent profitability of a universe of technical rules must be judged against the data snooping implied by searching over it, a point that applies with greater force when ML is layered on top.

Machine learning in trading. Gu et al. (2020) document substantial gains from tree ensembles and neural networks in predicting the cross-section of returns under strict train/validation/test separation. Krauss et al. (2017) and Fischer and Krauss (2018) apply deep networks, gradient boosting and random forests to statistical arbitrage. Closer to our setting, Lim et al. (2019) learn trend-following positions and sizes end-to-end with deep networks. López de Prado (2018) proposed meta-labelling: a secondary classifier that decides whether to act on a primary model’s signal and how much to bet, and Joubert (2022) formalises the framework. Our stage S1 is a meta-labelling filter and stage S3 a meta-labelling sizer; the contribution here is not the technique but its controlled evaluation against 39 primary rules, alongside other insertion points.

Backtest overfitting and multiple testing. White (2000)’s Reality Check and Hansen (2005)’s test of superior predictive ability (SPA) test whether the best of many models beats a benchmark. Romano and Wolf (2005) provide stepdown family-wise error control. Benjamini and Hochberg (1995) and Benjamini and Yekutieli (2001) control the false discovery rate. Ledoit and Wolf (2008) provide robust tests of Sharpe-ratio differences. Bailey and López de Prado (2014) propose the deflated Sharpe ratio, and Bailey et al. (2017) the probability of backtest overfitting (PBO) estimated by combinatorially symmetric cross-validation. Harvey et al. (2016) argue for higher significance hurdles in factor research, and Arnott et al. (2019) set out a backtesting protocol for the ML era. We apply this toolkit to a search of 39 strategies × 5 stages × 5 models.

The gap. What is missing is a controlled, stage-by-stage measurement. The question is not whether an ML trading model can be profitable, but how much ML adds to a given, published trend rule, at which point in the pipeline, and with what statistical confidence once the search over models and configurations is accounted for.

3. Data

3.1 Universe

The benchmark universe consists of 174 instruments with daily bars, totalling 1,100,307 instrument-days. It covers 34 equity indices across five regions; 37 continuous futures contracts (equity, interest rates, energy, metals, agriculture, softs, livestock and currencies); 7 G10 currency majors, 21 G10 crosses and 11 emerging-market currency pairs; 15 large-capitalisation cryptocurrencies; and 49 US-listed ETFs (equity, country, sector, rates, credit, commodity, real estate and currency). The universe deliberately avoids constituent selection: indices, continuous futures, currencies, ETFs and long-lived cryptocurrencies are not chosen because they survived, which limits survivorship bias.

Two further panels are kept out of the benchmark. Binance spot cryptocurrency data at one-hour and four-hour bars are used to test sensitivity to the sampling frequency. Single stocks from the current S&P 100 and NIFTY 50 constituents are survivorship-biased by construction and are used only as a labelled robustness run. Over all panels and frequencies we use 259 cleaned series with 2,670,334 bars (Table 1). Data come from Yahoo Finance, the Binance public API and FRED.

3.2 Cleaning and validation

Cleaning is mechanical and identical for all instruments. Duplicate dates and missing or non-positive closes are dropped. Isolated bad prints, defined as log returns larger than 12 robust (MAD-based) standard deviations that are immediately reversed on the next bar, are removed (625 cases). Inconsistent bars in which the high or low lies inside the open–close range are widened to include the open and close (16,962 cases, mostly in Yahoo currency data). Series with holes longer than one month are trimmed to the segment after the last hole. Three requested series could not be used: sugar futures (no usable history), the US dollar index future and one Indian stock that is no longer listed on the data source.

We validate the data by comparing overlapping series from different sources (Appendix Table C1). Daily Yahoo and Binance bitcoin returns correlate at 0.99; the S&P 500 index and the SPY ETF at 0.985 (0.995 weekly); and domestic index–ETF pairs between 0.96 and 0.99. Currency spot rates and currency futures correlate weakly at the daily frequency (about 0.4) because of differing fixing times, but at 0.86 at the weekly frequency. These checks confirm that the series are usable for a comparative study. Because the rule-based baseline and every ML variant are evaluated on the same bars, data imperfections affect both sides of each comparison.

4. Methodology

4.1 Rule-based baselines

We implement 39 published or widely used trend-following rules spanning eight families: moving-average crossovers (7 rules), price-versus-moving-average filters (2), adaptive moving averages (6), channel breakouts (5), volatility stops (4), time-series momentum (4), oscillator-based trend rules (6) and directional-movement systems (5). Appendix Table A1 lists every rule with its reference and parameters. Each rule maps the OHLC history up to the close of bar tt to a target position pt∈[−1,1]p_t \in [-1, 1], using the originator’s default parameters. Rules are long/short (stop-and-reverse, or with a flat state where the original has one); the two long-only exceptions, Faber (2007)’s ten-month moving average and Antonacci (2014)’s absolute momentum, follow their sources. For parameter adaptation (stage S5) each rule also has a slow variant, with all lookbacks doubled, and a fast variant, with lookbacks halved.

4.2 Features

The ML models see 56 features drawn from ten indicator families (Appendix Table A2). They include momentum over 5 to 252 bars; five volatility estimators (close-to-close, Parkinson (1980), Garman and Klass (1980), Rogers and Satchell (1991) and Yang and Zhang (2000)); ADX/DMI, Aroon, RSI, MACD, stochastics, CCI, TRIX and Vortex; Kaufman’s efficiency ratio and the choppiness index; regression-slope t-statistics and R2R^2; distances to SMA, KAMA, Parabolic SAR, SuperTrend and Ichimoku levels; Bollinger, Keltner and Donchian channel positions; the variance ratio of Lo and MacKinlay (1988) and the first-order autocorrelation; higher moments; drawdown state; and four volume features. Every feature is dimensionless (a ratio, a bounded oscillator or a volatility-scaled distance), so a single pooled model can serve all instruments.

The rule-specific stages (S1, S3, S4) additionally receive six trade-state features: the current position’s sign and size, the log age of the current trade, the volatility-scaled open profit or loss, and the volatility-scaled result and log length of the previous completed trade. These are causal functions of prices and of the rule’s own signals, but they are not technical indicators. Stage S5 receives the current positions of the three variants instead, and the regime model (S2) receives indicators only.

Causality. For every one of the 56 features and 39 rules, a unit test verifies that the value at bar tt is identical whether it is computed on the full history or on the history truncated at tt. This test caught one subtle look-ahead during development: a month-end rebalancing flag set on the last available bar caused a truncated series to rebalance on a day on which the full series would not. The flag is now derived from the calendar.

4.4 Models

Every stage trains five models whose hyper-parameters are fixed a priori: L2-regularised logistic regression; a random forest (Breiman 2001) of 100 bagged trees of depth 7 with 15% of features per tree; extremely randomised trees (Geurts et al. 2006); LightGBM gradient boosting (Ke et al. 2017) with 200 trees, 15 leaves and a learning rate of 0.03; and a two-layer perceptron (32 and 16 units) with early stopping. Minimum leaf sizes scale with the size of the training set. Models are pooled across all instruments of a panel, with one model per strategy, stage and fold. Training rows are sub-sampled at the label horizon so that labels from the same instrument do not overlap.

4.5 Walk-forward validation with purging

Test years run from 2006 to 2026, with one fold per calendar year. For test year YY:

  1. models are trained on all data before Y−2Y-2 (expanding window);
  2. the model and the intervention intensity are selected on the validation block [Y−2,Y)[Y-2, Y);
  3. the frozen choice is applied to year YY.

A training row is admitted only if its label is fully realised, including a five-bar embargo, before the validation block starts, and validation labels must be realised before the test year begins (López de Prado 2018). The selected configuration is the (model, intensity) pair with the highest validation-period portfolio Sharpe ratio. Each alternative model keeps its own validation-chosen intensity, so all five models are also reported out of sample, but nothing out of sample feeds back into any choice. The out-of-sample (OOS) record is the concatenation of the test years, from 2 January 2006 to 6 October 2026. Two alternative designs are run separately: a rolling ten-year training window and a single 2013 hold-out split with no retraining.

4.7 Performance measures

Returns are returns on notional exposure, following the excess-return convention of the managed-futures literature, so no risk-free rate is subtracted. The annualised Sharpe ratio is SR=AR‾/σ̂R\mathrm{SR} = \sqrt{A}\,\bar R / \hat\sigma_R, with AA the number of bars per year. The quantity of interest is the paired difference ΔSR=SR(ML)−SR(rule),(5)\Delta \mathrm{SR} = \mathrm{SR}(\text{ML}) - \mathrm{SR}(\text{rule}), \qquad\qquad (5) computed on identical days. We also report CAGR, total return, volatility, the Sortino and Calmar ratios, maximum drawdown, trade win rate, profit factor, annual turnover, cost drag, exposure, the share of positive calendar years and a stability measure (the signed R2R^2 of log equity on time).

4.8 Statistical inference

For each strategy and stage we test H0:SR(ML)=SR(rule)H_0\!: \mathrm{SR}(\text{ML}) = \mathrm{SR}(\text{rule}) on the paired daily OOS returns. At the portfolio level we use a paired circular block bootstrap (Politis and Romano 1992) with 20-day blocks and 2,000 resamples, which preserves both the serial dependence and the cross-correlation of the two return series. At the instrument level we use the HAC test of Ledoit and Wolf (2008). The headline family consists of the 39 × 5 = 195 portfolio-level tests, reported raw and with Holm (family-wise) and Benjamini–Hochberg (false discovery rate) adjustment. Within each strategy, Hansen’s SPA test and White’s Reality Check ask whether any of the 25 stage × model configurations beats the baseline, and Romano–Wolf stepdown p-values are computed. To diagnose selection overfitting we compute the probability of backtest overfitting across each strategy’s 26 configurations (combinatorially symmetric cross-validation with 16 blocks) and the deflated Sharpe ratio of the ex-post best configuration. Finally, an ordinary least squares regression with instrument-clustered standard errors relates instrument-level ΔSR\Delta\mathrm{SR} to the stage, model, strategy family, asset class and the baseline’s own Sharpe ratio.

4.9 Controls: placebo, random-walk null and feature ablation

Placebo. Removing trades or resizing positions can change a Sharpe ratio even without any information. Each decision rule is therefore re-run with the same validation-chosen intensity but with uniform random numbers in place of the model predictions (20 draws per strategy and stage). The difference between the ML and placebo results measures what the model learns.

Random-walk null. If any component leaked future information, ML would “improve” rules even where there is nothing to predict. The whole pipeline is run unchanged on 60 synthetic random-walk instruments with GARCH-tt returns and the same costs, for 8 representative strategies.

Feature ablation. Stages S1, S3 and S4 are re-run with the feature set restricted to one indicator family at a time (momentum, volatility, trend, oscillator/channel, other) and to no indicator at all (“context only”: vol_20 plus the trade-state features). For computational reasons the ablation uses a lighter protocol (two models, logistic regression and LightGBM, retraining every three years) on 15 representative strategies covering all eight families, so the blocks are compared with each other and with a full-feature reference run under the same protocol.

4.10 Reproducibility and experiment registry

Every experiment writes rows to an SQLite registry that links strategy → indicators → market → instrument → dataset hash → ML stage → ML model (with per-fold selection counts) → validation design → backtest configuration → metrics → stored return series, together with a hash of the source code that produced it. All tables in this paper are generated from that registry. Data downloads, cleaning, experiments, statistics and the paper itself are reproduced by scripts, and every component (universe, data source, frequency, features, strategies, models, stages, validation scheme, execution, costs and sizing) can be swapped through configuration.

5. Results

All results in this section are out of sample, from January 2006 to October 2026, on 174 instruments with next-open execution and 1× costs, unless stated otherwise. Δ\DeltaSR is the annualised Sharpe ratio of the ML-enhanced strategy minus that of its own rule-based baseline on identical days. The selected model is the validation winner, never the ex-post best.

5.1 The rule-based baselines

After costs, the 39 published rules earn an average portfolio Sharpe ratio of 0.01 (median −0.01) over 2006–2026, and only 18 of the 39 have a positive Sharpe ratio (Appendix Table B1). The best rules are slow, long-only or monthly: Faber (2007)’s ten-month moving average reaches 0.85 and absolute momentum 0.84. The worst is the fractal adaptive moving average at −0.83. This is consistent with the well-documented weak decade for trend following after 2009. Fast crossovers and adaptive moving averages pay substantial costs for whipsaw trades: the fastest rules have annual portfolio turnover of 50 to 95 times their capital. Each instrument is scaled to 10% volatility, but the equal-capital portfolio of 174 weakly correlated instruments has a realised volatility of only 2–3% a year. CAGRs are therefore small in absolute terms and scale linearly with any leverage applied; the Sharpe, Sortino and Calmar ratios are the comparable quantities.

The answer depends sharply on where ML is inserted. Trade selection (S1) and position sizing (S3) give the largest and most reliable improvements: mean Δ\DeltaSR of +0.47 and +0.44, with 38 of the 39 strategies improved in both cases, and significant improvements for 31 and 33 strategies after BH adjustment (10 and 16 after the stricter Holm correction). The strategy-agnostic trend-regime gate (S2) does not help: its mean Δ\DeltaSR is −0.01 and only 46% of strategies improve. Predicting that a market will trend is not the same as predicting that a given rule’s current position will pay; only the latter, strategy-specific target produced gains. S2 is also the only stage at which validation-based selection actively hurts. Every individual model except the MLP has a slightly positive mean OOS Δ\DeltaSR (Table 5), but the fold-by-fold validation winner does not: the gate’s effects are too small and unstable for a two-year validation block to rank them reliably.

Across all 195 tests, 27 improvements survive the Holm correction and 84 survive BH, while only one significant deterioration survives BH. Within each strategy, Hansen’s SPA test rejects the hypothesis that no ML configuration beats the rule for 38 of the 39 strategies (38 after Holm adjustment across strategies); White’s Reality Check rejects it for 37. 5.4 Portfolio versus single instruments

At the instrument level, where the rules trade unit size exactly as published, the improvements are real but much smaller. Across the 6,786 strategy–instrument pairs, S1 raises the Sharpe ratio by +0.12 on average and improves 77% of pairs, but only 86 pairs (1.3%) are individually significant after BH adjustment. S3 gives +0.12 (76% improved, 264 significant), S4 +0.08, S5 +0.04 and S2 +0.01, with 12 significant deteriorations for S2. Single-instrument Sharpe ratios are noisy over twenty years, and the portfolio-level gains arise because small, consistent per-instrument improvements accumulate across 174 weakly correlated instruments. A practitioner who trades one market should expect the instrument-level effect, not the portfolio-level one.

5.10 Overfitting diagnostics

Across each strategy’s 26 OOS configurations (baseline plus 5 stages × 5 models), the probability of backtest overfitting is low: the median PBO is 0.009 and no strategy exceeds 0.5 (Appendix Table B2). The ex-post best configuration is almost always an S3 or S1 model. However, the absolute Sharpe ratio of the best configuration survives deflation for 25 trials (deflated Sharpe ratio above 0.95) for only 9 of the 39 strategies. ML makes weak rules markedly better, but most ML-enhanced rules are still not convincingly profitable on their own after costs over 2006–2026. The improvement is significant; the resulting stand-alone strategy usually is not.

8. Discussion

8.1 Answer to the research question

How much? It depends on the stage. Out of sample over 2006–2026, ML that selects trades raises the Sharpe ratio of the average trend rule by 0.47, and ML position sizing by 0.44, from a baseline of 0.01. ML exits add 0.28 and parameter adaptation 0.16, while a strategy-agnostic regime gate adds nothing. In a diversified composite of all 39 rules the Sharpe ratio rises from 0.01 to 0.60 (S1) or 0.57 (S3), and the maximum drawdown falls from −9.5% to −4.1%.

Is it statistically significant? Yes for S1 and S3, and partly for S4. After BH adjustment over the 195 strategy × stage tests, 31 (S1), 33 (S3) and 15 (S4) of the 39 strategies improve significantly; after Holm adjustment, 10, 16 and 1. Hansen’s SPA test rejects “no ML configuration beats the rule” for 38 of 39 strategies, the probability of backtest overfitting is low, the random-prediction placebo is negative or null, and the random-walk experiment shows no broad improvement.

Under what conditions is it robust? The improvement is robust to the validation design (expanding, rolling, single hold-out), to the time period (positive in every five-year sub-period for S1, S3, S4 and S5), and to costs, execution timing and sizing. It holds for equity indices, ETFs, emerging-market currencies, cryptocurrencies and, less strongly, futures. It is largest for weak, high-turnover rules and smallest for slow, already-profitable rules. Tree ensembles are the best models and the neural network the weakest.

Where it is not robust. The improvement does not transfer to intraday frequencies for position sizing, it is absent for G10 currencies, and regime detection does not help in any daily design. Part of the trade-filtering gain is cost avoidance. Most importantly, the gain does not come from the technical indicators: a model that sees no indicator, only volatility and the state of the open trade, obtains the same improvement as one given 56 indicators.

8.2 Interpretation and implications

Three implications follow. First, for practitioners, the most productive use of ML on a trend rule is rule-specific: deciding whether to take a given trade and how large to make it. Strategy-agnostic forecasts of whether a market will trend did not help, even though the regime models carry some information (their placebo is strongly negative). The economically relevant target is the payoff of the rule’s own position.

Second, the evidence that ML “improves trend following” should be read as evidence that a learned trade-management layer improves it, not as evidence that the indicator library has predictive content. This matters both for the design of such systems (a parsimonious state representation suffices) and for the interpretation of the many studies that attribute ML gains to elaborate indicator sets. It also suggests that part of what the models learn might be replicable by simpler, hand-written rules, which is the most important open question left by this study.

Third, statistical and economic significance diverge. A robust improvement of 0.4–0.5 in Sharpe ratio, measured against baselines that earn about zero, still leaves most individual ML-enhanced rules short of convincing stand-alone profitability once the search is deflated. The improvement is largest precisely where the rules are weakest. The case for ML is strongest in diversified portfolios of rules and instruments, where small, consistent per-instrument gains accumulate, and weakest for a single rule on a single market.

9. Limitations

Data quality. The data come from free vendors. Continuous futures are not roll-adjusted, indices exclude dividends, currency returns exclude carry and cryptocurrency prices are cross-venue aggregates. These affect the baseline and the ML variants identically, so the differences are less exposed than the levels, but absolute Sharpe ratios should be read with these caveats.

Costs. Costs are per-asset-class assumptions. Market impact is not modelled, which matters for large capital in the less liquid futures and emerging-market currencies.

Fixed hyper-parameters. Each model family is represented by one a-priori configuration. Tuning on validation data could change the model ranking, but would also enlarge the search that the multiple-testing corrections must cover.

Mechanism. The ablation shows that the gains do not require technical indicators, but it cannot establish what the models learn from the trade state and volatility. We did not compare the models with hand-written volatility-scaled trailing stops or re-entry rules.

Ablation scope. The single-family and context-only blocks use a lighter protocol (S1, S3 and S4; two models; three-year retraining; 15 representative strategies).

Pooled models. One model is fitted per strategy and stage across all instruments. Per-asset-class models are a natural extension.

History. The OOS period starts in 2006. Longer histories (pre-1990 futures, century-long index series) would cover more trend regimes.

Survivorship. The benchmark universe avoids constituent selection. The single-stock run is survivorship-biased by construction and is reported only as such.

10. Conclusion

We measured how much machine learning improves 39 established trend-following rules, stage by stage, on a multi-asset panel with purged walk-forward validation, realistic execution and costs, and inference that accounts for the size of the search. ML that selects trades or sizes positions improves the average rule by 0.44–0.47 in Sharpe ratio, significantly for most rules, robustly across validation designs, periods and backtest assumptions, and beyond what a random-prediction placebo achieves. ML exits help less, parameter adaptation little, and strategy-agnostic regime detection not at all. The gains concentrate in weak rules, fail at intraday frequencies for position sizing and are absent for G10 currencies. Most importantly, they do not depend on technical indicators: volatility and the state of the open trade suffice. The natural next step is to test whether hand-written trade-management rules can recover the learned improvement, which would tell us whether ML adds something genuinely new to trend following or rediscovers rules that traders could write down themselves.
